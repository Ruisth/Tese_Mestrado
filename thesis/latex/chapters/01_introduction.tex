\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Context and Motivation}
\label{sec:intro-context}

\noindent Consumer wearable devices --- smartwatches, smart rings and sensorised
clothing --- continuously produce streams of personal measurements such as heart
rate, skin temperature, blood-oxygen saturation and motion. In the prevailing
deployment model, this telemetry is transmitted to vendor-operated cloud
platforms, where it is stored, processed and exposed back to the consumer
through proprietary applications. Digital twins --- synchronised digital
representations of physical assets --- offer a principled way to organise such
device state and make it queryable through uniform interfaces
\cite{fuller2020digital,minerva2020digital}. At the same time, edge computing
argues for moving computation and data handling closer to where data is
produced, reducing dependence on wide-area connectivity and keeping sensitive
data under local control \cite{shi2016edge}.

This dissertation is developed in the context of the Consumer-Controlled
Digital Twin Architecture (C2DTA) proposed by Pinto et
al.~\cite{pinto2026c2dta}, which places the consumer, rather than the device
vendor, in control of the digital twins of their smart devices. Within that
broader architecture, the component studied here is the \emph{edge gateway}:
the household-side platform that receives device telemetry, validates it and
materialises it as digital twins on local, self-hosted infrastructure.

The engineering motivation is concrete. If a household edge gateway is to be
credible, its operating platform must be \emph{reproducible} --- buildable from
a pinned, versioned configuration rather than assembled by hand --- and its
digital-twin services must run on the ARM64 architecture that dominates
low-power edge hardware. Whether an open-source digital-twin stack, hosted on
such a platform, can correctly and reliably absorb the concurrent telemetry of
several heterogeneous wearables, and at what resource cost, is an empirical
question that requires systematic evidence.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:intro-problem}

\noindent The problem addressed by this dissertation is the absence of a
reproducible, evidence-backed reference implementation and evaluation of an
ARM64 edge gateway for wearable digital twins. Prior work in the C2DTA line
evaluated a single smartwatch profile at one message per second on an x86
virtual machine \cite{pinto2026c2dta}; it did not demonstrate a Yocto-built
platform, ARM64 deployment, concurrent heterogeneous devices, load or
robustness scenarios, resource-consumption profiling, or long-duration
stability. Component-level studies exist --- digital-twin frameworks at the edge
\cite{picone2023flexible}, MQTT broker comparisons \cite{koziolek2020mqtt},
container overhead on constrained devices
\cite{morabito2017virtualization,felter2015updated} --- but they do not compose
into a validated, reproducible gateway platform for this use case.

\section{Research Gap}
\label{sec:intro-gap}

\noindent Within the searches executed for this work (Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}
describes the review protocol and its limits), we did not identify a published,
reproducible evaluation of an edge gateway that combines (i) an operating
system image built with the Yocto Project from a pinned configuration for
ARM64, (ii) a containerised digital-twin stack based on Eclipse Ditto with an
MQTT ingestion path, and (iii) a pre-registered experimental protocol covering
correctness, reliability, latency, throughput, resource consumption and
long-duration stability under concurrent multi-device wearable telemetry. This
combination is the gap this dissertation addresses. The claim is deliberately
bounded: it is relative to the literature review protocol actually executed,
not an absolute statement about all published work.

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:intro-rqs}

\noindent The dissertation answers three research questions, fixed in the
project plan and unchanged since its adoption:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*.}]
  \item \textit{How can a reproducible Yocto-based ARM64 edge gateway be
        designed to host containerised digital-twin services?}
  \item \textit{To what extent can the gateway ingest and materialise
        concurrent synthetic telemetry from three wearable-device types
        correctly and reliably?}
  \item \textit{What latency, throughput and resource-consumption trade-offs
        constrain deployment of the proposed platform on an ARM64 edge-class
        environment?}
\end{enumerate}

RQ1 is a design and constructive question, answered by the artefact itself and
by functional evidence of its reproducibility. RQ2 and RQ3 are empirical
questions, answered exclusively with data produced by the experimental
campaign described in Chapters~\ref{ch:methodology}
and~\ref{ch:evaluation}.

\section{Objectives}
\label{sec:intro-objectives}

\noindent The overall objective is to conceive, implement and evaluate a
reproducible ARM64 edge gateway, based on the Yocto Project and containerised
services, capable of receiving concurrent synthetic telemetry from three
wearable-device types, validating the events, and materialising them as
digital twins in Eclipse Ditto. This decomposes into the following specific
objectives:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Define versioned, normative interfaces for the telemetry path: MQTT
        topic layout, a common event envelope, per-device JSON Schemas, and
        \ac{WoT} Thing Descriptions.
  \item Build a minimal, reproducible operating-system image (\texttt{egw-image})
        with the Yocto Project Scarthgap \ac{LTS} line from a pinned
        \texttt{kas} manifest, validated functionally on emulated ARM64
        (\texttt{qemuarm64}) with systemd, networking and an \ac{OCI} container
        runtime.
  \item Deploy a minimal ARM64 digital-twin stack --- Mosquitto with \ac{TLS},
        Eclipse Ditto (gateway, policies, things), MongoDB and a purpose-built
        ingestion controller --- with all images verified for
        \texttt{linux/arm64} and pinned by digest.
  \item Implement a deterministic, seedable \ac{CLI} simulator for the three
        wearable types and six scenarios (\texttt{smoke}, \texttt{nominal},
        \texttt{load-sweep}, \texttt{dropout-reconnect},
        \texttt{invalid-payload}, \texttt{soak}).
  \item Execute a pre-registered experimental campaign on a native ARM64
        environment and analyse it with a single reproducible pipeline from
        raw data to tables and figures.
  \item Deliver a reproducibility package: code, configuration, manifests,
        raw data, checksums and analysis scripts.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:intro-contributions}

\noindent As designed, the dissertation contributes the following artefacts
and evidence. Each contribution stands or falls with the evidence presented in
Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}; none is claimed as demonstrated until that
evidence exists.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A reproducible platform recipe.} A pinned Yocto/Scarthgap
        configuration (\texttt{kas} manifest and custom layer) producing a
        bootable ARM64 image with a container runtime, together with the
        documented procedure to rebuild it from a clean checkout.
  \item \textbf{A contract-first ingestion architecture.} Normative interfaces
        (topic layout, event envelope, JSON Schemas, Thing Descriptions, twin
        model) and a controller design providing validation, idempotent twin
        updates and structured evidence logging.
  \item \textbf{A deterministic wearable workload generator.} A seedable
        simulator producing reproducible multi-device telemetry across six
        scenarios, suitable for reuse in comparable studies.
  \item \textbf{A pre-registered, reproducible evaluation.} A protocol with
        fixed metrics, run counts and statistical rules, and a campaign whose
        raw data, manifests and analysis pipeline are archived and
        re-executable.
  \item \textbf{An evidence-bounded characterisation} of the latency,
        throughput, resource-consumption and stability envelope of the
        proposed gateway on an ARM64 edge-class environment.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and Delimitation}
\label{sec:intro-scope}

\noindent The scope is deliberately narrow so that every claim can be backed
by evidence produced within the project window:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Telemetry is \emph{synthetic}, produced by the deterministic
        simulator; no physical wearables or human-subject data are used.
  \item Functional platform validation uses QEMU emulation
        (\texttt{qemuarm64}); performance evaluation uses a native ARM64
        virtual machine. No performance conclusions are drawn from emulation,
        and no physical single-board hardware is evaluated.
  \item The digital-twin stack is a minimal core (Mosquitto, Ditto gateway,
        policies and things, MongoDB, controller); Ditto search, connectivity
        and user-interface services are excluded.
  \item \ac{SSI} and blockchain elements of the broader C2DTA vision are
        discussed as context only (Chapter~\ref{ch:background}); they are
        neither implemented nor evaluated here, and no claims are made about
        them.
  \item Exactly three wearable-device types are modelled; graphical
        interfaces, Bluetooth connectivity, over-the-air updates and energy
        measurements are out of scope.
\end{itemize}

\section{Document Structure}
\label{sec:intro-structure}

\noindent The remainder of this document is organised as follows.
Chapter~\ref{ch:background} reviews the background technologies and related
work on edge computing, digital twins, embedded Linux, containerisation, MQTT
and the Web of Things, and positions this work against the C2DTA line.
Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} presents the research methodology: a Design
Science Research framing, the literature-review protocol, the mapping from
research questions to methods and metrics, and the experimental protocol.
Chapter~\ref{ch:architecture} describes the architecture and implementation of
the gateway as realised in the project repository.
Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} reports the evaluation and discusses the results
against the research questions, including threats to validity.
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions} summarises contributions, limitations and future
work.
