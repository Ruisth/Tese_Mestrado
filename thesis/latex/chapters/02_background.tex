\chapter{Background and Related Work}
\label{ch:background}

\noindent This chapter provides the technical background for the artefact and
positions the dissertation against related work. It is organised
topic-by-topic; a final section synthesises the comparison and restates the
gap. The underlying literature search is governed by the structured

official documentation, and candidates identified through preliminary

\section{Edge Computing}
\label{sec:bg-edge}

\subsection{From Cloud to Edge}
\label{sec:bg-edge-motivation}

\noindent Edge computing designates the paradigm in which computation and
storage are placed near the sources of data, at or close to the network edge,
rather than exclusively in centralised data centres. Shi et al.\ articulate
its canonical motivations: latency reduction, bandwidth economy, availability
under intermittent connectivity, and the ability to keep sensitive data close
to its origin \cite{shi2016edge}. Satyanarayanan traces the paradigm's
emergence from earlier work on cloudlets and proximity-based offloading, and
identifies the same recurring drivers --- end-to-end latency,
bandwidth-induced cost, privacy, and resilience when the wide-area network is
degraded --- as the forces that pull computation out of the data centre
\cite{satyanarayanan2017emergence}.

For continuously sensed \emph{personal} data these motivations compound. The
telemetry emitted by consumer wearables --- heart rate, skin temperature,
blood-oxygen saturation, movement --- is intimate by nature: from it,
inferences about health, sleep, stress and daily routine are possible. A
deployment model in which every reading crosses the wide-area network to a
vendor-operated platform therefore maximises both the exposure surface of the
data and the dependence of basic functionality on external connectivity. An
edge deployment inverts this default: raw measurements are ingested,
validated and materialised on infrastructure inside the household, and any
outward sharing becomes a deliberate, selective act rather than a
precondition of the service. This data-locality argument, together with the
latency and availability arguments of the general edge literature
\cite{shi2016edge,satyanarayanan2017emergence}, is the primary motivation for
the artefact studied in this dissertation.

\subsection{Edge Gateways for Personal Sensing}
\label{sec:bg-edge-gateways}

\noindent Between resource-poor sensing devices and any local or remote
service sits the \emph{edge gateway}: a mains-powered, always-on device that
terminates device-facing protocols, applies validation and access control,
hosts local processing, and mediates any communication with the outside
world. In the consumer setting the gateway is the natural place to
concentrate the household's trust: it is the single component that sees all
device telemetry, and the component whose software provenance and
configuration must therefore be most defensible. This reading places two
engineering requirements at the centre of the present work: the gateway's
operating platform should be \emph{reproducible} --- buildable from a pinned,
versioned description rather than assembled by hand --- and its services
should run natively on the ARM64 architecture that dominates low-power edge
hardware. Recent work has also begun to address the operational robustness of
edge-hosted twin services themselves; Neagu et al., for example, propose a
high-availability standby mechanism for digital-twin nodes in
resource-constrained edge environments, evaluated through fault-injection
experiments \cite{neagu2026odt}. A dedicated pass over the edge-gateway
architecture literature is part of the pending institutional search plan
(review axis~A, Section~\ref{sec:method-review}).

\subsection{Benchmarking Edge Systems}
\label{sec:bg-edge-bench}

\noindent Evaluating systems at the edge raises methodological questions that
differ from cloud benchmarking: workloads must be representative of edge
traffic patterns, the systems under test are small enough that measurement
overhead can distort results, and heterogeneous hardware makes repetition
discipline and statistical reporting essential. Varghese et al.\ survey the
edge performance-benchmarking landscape and catalogue these concerns,
including metric selection, workload generation and the placement of the load
generator relative to the system under test \cite{varghese2021survey}. Those
concerns shape the experimental protocol of this dissertation
(Section~\ref{sec:method-protocol}): the workload generator runs outside the
system under test, the primary latency metric is computed from monotonic
clocks inside a single process, the statistical unit is the run, and all
conditions are fixed before the campaign.

\section{Digital Twins}
\label{sec:bg-dt}

\subsection{Origin and Concept}
\label{sec:bg-dt-origin}

\noindent The digital-twin concept originates in product lifecycle
management: Grieves and Vickers describe a virtual construct that mirrors a
physical system across its life, fed by data flowing between the physical and
virtual sides, and used to anticipate emergent behaviour that would otherwise
surface only in operation \cite{grieves2017digital}. Already in that original
formulation the twin has three constituents --- the physical entity, its
virtual counterpart, and the data connection that keeps the two aligned ---
and it is the third constituent, the sustained synchronisation channel, that
turns a static model into a twin. In its modern \ac{IoT} reading, a digital
twin is a synchronised, addressable digital counterpart of a physical entity,
exposed to applications through uniform interfaces. Fuller
et al.\ survey the enabling technologies and open research questions of the
field, and note the persistent ambiguity of the term across domains
\cite{fuller2020digital}. Minerva et al.\ provide the most systematic
treatment for the \ac{IoT} context, analysing definitions, application
scenarios and architectural models, and characterising the twin as a
continuously updated state representation whose value lies in being
queryable, composable and decoupled from the physical device's availability
\cite{minerva2020digital}. It is this \ac{IoT} reading --- the twin as a live,
addressable state resource --- that the present work adopts: each wearable is
represented by one twin whose properties are updated from telemetry and can
be read back through a stable \ac{API}.

Sustaining that reading in software implies a recurring set of platform
obligations, whatever the domain: representing twin state in a structured,
versionable form; giving each twin a stable identity and address; keeping
state synchronised with the device under unreliable, at-least-once
delivery; controlling who may read and write which parts of the state; and
exposing the result through interfaces that applications can rely on. These
obligations are exactly the substrate that digital-twin \emph{platforms}
exist to provide, and they set the evaluation frame for the next
subsection: the question is not whether a twin can be built --- it
demonstrably can --- but which platform obligations a given system
discharges, on what infrastructure, and at what cost.

\subsection{Digital-Twin Platforms for the IoT}
\label{sec:bg-ditto}

\noindent Building twins directly on application code is possible, but a
platform layer --- managing twin state, identity, access control and
interfaces --- avoids re-inventing that substrate per project. The platform
used by this work's artefact is \emph{Eclipse Ditto}, an open-source
framework, hosted by the Eclipse Foundation, for managing digital twins as
addressable web resources \cite{eclipse2026ditto}. Ditto's model organises a
twin (a \emph{thing}) into free-form \emph{attributes} and structured
\emph{features}, each feature carrying a set of properties; things are
addressed by namespaced identifiers and guarded by \emph{policies},
fine-grained authorisation documents that bind subjects to permissions on
thing and policy resources. State is manipulated through \ac{HTTP} and
messaging \ac{API}s, including partial updates via \ac{JSON} merge patch ---
the mechanism the ingestion controller of this work uses to fold each
telemetry event into its twin (Chapter~\ref{ch:architecture}). Ditto is
deliberately \emph{not} an end-to-end \ac{IoT} platform: device connectivity
is delegated to companion components (typically a message broker), while
Ditto provides the twin layer. Architecturally it is a set of microservices
--- gateway, policies, things, search and connectivity, over MongoDB
persistence --- that can be deployed selectively; this modularity is what
makes a minimal-core deployment possible on resource-limited hardware. The
artefact of this dissertation includes only the gateway, policies and things
services, with MongoDB, on Ditto 3.9.4 \cite{eclipse2026ditto}. Ditto also
documents native \ac{WoT} support, introduced as an optional integration in
version 2.4.0 and considered stable and active by default since version 3.0.0
\cite{eclipse2026ditto}, which connects the twin model to the standards
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:bg-wot}.

Ditto is not the only option, and the choice deserves justification against
alternatives representing the main platform families. \emph{Azure Digital
Twins} represents the hyperscaler platform-as-a-service family: twins are
nodes in a cloud-hosted twin graph, modelled in the Digital Twins Definition
Language and integrated with the vendor's event and analytics services
\cite{microsoft2026azuredt}. As vendor documentation this source is grey
literature and is used here only as engineering context; the disqualifying
property for the present use case is architectural, not qualitative: a
cloud-hosted twin service reintroduces exactly the wide-area dependence and
third-party data custody that the edge deployment is meant to remove.
\emph{WLDT} (White Label Digital Twin) represents the library family: Picone
et al.\ provide a general-purpose library with which twins are instantiated
programmatically as lightweight software processes co-located with the
application \cite{picone2021wldt}. This maximises flexibility and minimises
footprint, but leaves state management, access control and external
\ac{API}s to the adopter --- precisely the substrate this project prefers to
inherit from a platform. \emph{OpenTwins} represents the composed-framework
family: Robles et al.\ assemble an open-source stack for compositional twins
with 3D visualisation and machine-learning integration, built around Eclipse
Ditto as the twin core together with messaging, time-series storage and
dashboard components, deployed on Kubernetes and demonstrated on an
Industry~4.0 use case \cite{robles2023opentwins}. OpenTwins corroborates
Ditto's position as the de facto open-source twin core, while illustrating
the opposite sizing philosophy: where OpenTwins adds services around Ditto,
the present work subtracts them, retaining only the minimal subset that the
research questions require. Self-hostability, an explicit policy model, a
documented \ac{HTTP} \ac{API} with merge-patch semantics, availability of
ARM64 container images, and the ability to run a reduced service set were the
deciding criteria.

\subsection{Digital Twins at the Edge}
\label{sec:bg-dt-edge}

\noindent Most published twin deployments assume cloud hosting; a smaller
body of work studies twins on constrained edge infrastructure. Picone et al.\
design and evaluate a modular \emph{edge digital twin} architecture in which
twins are lightweight replications of physical devices providing a digital
abstraction layer close to the devices themselves, with an experimental
analysis of the approach on resource-limited hardware across multiple
\ac{IoT} application contexts \cite{picone2023flexible}. This is the closest
methodological precedent for the present work's evaluation: it demonstrates
that hosting and measuring twins at the edge is feasible and worth
characterising quantitatively. Complementarily, the operational-robustness
line represented by Neagu et al.\ treats the edge twin itself as
infrastructure to be hardened, contributing failover mechanisms and
fault-injection evidence \cite{neagu2026odt}. Neither line, within the
sources examined so far, addresses the platform question studied here: what
it costs, on a reproducibly built ARM64 gateway, to ingest and materialise
concurrent multi-device wearable telemetry on an off-the-shelf twin platform.

\section{Consumer Digital Twins and the C2DTA Line}
\label{sec:bg-c2dta}

\noindent Industrial digital twins serve an organisation that owns both the
asset and the data. Consumer digital twins invert the ownership question:
the subject of the data is a private person, and the twin aggregates
measurements that are sensitive individually and more sensitive in
combination. The Consumer-Controlled Digital Twin Architecture (C2DTA) of
Pinto et al.\ addresses this setting directly: blockchain technology is used
to give consumers control over their smart devices' digital twins and the
data those twins accumulate \cite{pinto2026c2dta}. The architecture spans
consumer-side gateways, twin services and distributed-ledger components for
authorisation and auditability. Its published evaluation instantiates a
single smartwatch profile at one message per second on an x86 virtual
machine \cite{pinto2026c2dta}.

Consumer control, as C2DTA construes it, has an often-implicit
infrastructural precondition: control is only as strong as the custody of
the twin's primary instantiation. If the sole live copy of a person's twins
runs on third-party infrastructure, contractual and cryptographic controls
still operate inside someone else's failure and trust domain; a
household-side platform gives the control mechanisms a substrate the
consumer physically possesses. The maturity of that substrate is therefore
not an implementation detail of the C2DTA vision but a load-bearing
assumption of it --- and it is precisely the assumption this dissertation
sets out to engineer and measure.

This dissertation is positioned as the \emph{edge-platform layer} of that
line of work. It takes the gateway component of the C2DTA vision and defines
for it a substantially broader engineering and empirical programme: a
Yocto-defined, revision-pinned ARM64 operating platform; three concurrent
wearable types instead of one; six workload scenarios including fault
injection and a 24-hour soak; and per-container resource profiling under a
pre-specified protocol, frozen before data collection. Conversely, everything above the platform layer in
C2DTA --- ledger, identity, marketplace and consent mechanics --- is context
only here: none of it is implemented or evaluated, and no result in this
document supports or weakens claims about those components
(Section~\ref{sec:intro-scope}).

\section{Wearable Devices and Synthetic Workloads}
\label{sec:bg-wearables}
\label{sec:bg-dt-wearables}

\subsection{Wearable Sensing and Its Telemetry}
\label{sec:bg-wearables-sensing}

\noindent Consumer wearables span a spectrum of form factors --- wrist-worn
watches and bands, rings, and sensorised garments among them --- and a
corresponding spectrum of sensing modalities, from optical heart-rate
monitoring and skin-temperature sensing to blood-oxygen estimation and
inertial motion capture; Seneviratne et al.\ survey the device landscape, its
sensing capabilities and its systems challenges, including the prevailing
communication architecture in which devices deliver data through vendor
smartphone applications and cloud services rather than through open local
interfaces \cite{seneviratne2017wearables}. Real deployments produce
long-lived, structured, multi-signal streams: the PMData dataset, for
example, logged sixteen participants over five months with a consumer
smartwatch and a sports-logging application, accumulating continuous heart
rate, step, distance, calorie and sleep records alongside subjective
wellness reports \cite{thambawita2020pmdata}. Two properties of such
telemetry matter for a gateway platform: it is \emph{heterogeneous} (per
signal type in schema and in rate) and it is \emph{sustained} (devices emit
around the clock, so the platform's steady-state cost is the relevant cost).
The rates involved also differ by modality in a systematic way: episodic
wellness metrics such as skin temperature or blood-oxygen estimates are
produced at intervals of seconds to minutes; heart rate is tracked
effectively continuously, at aggregate rates of the order of one reading
per second; and inertial motion sensing operates at raw sampling rates one
to two orders of magnitude higher still, so that motion-oriented garments
dominate the message budget of any gateway that serves a mixed device
population \cite{seneviratne2017wearables}.

The three device types modelled in this work --- smartwatch, smart ring and
smart clothing --- were chosen to span that heterogeneity with nominal
emission rates covering nearly two orders of magnitude: continuous vitals
streaming at one message per second for the smartwatch (the same rate as the
published C2DTA evaluation profile \cite{pinto2026c2dta}), episodic wellness
measurements at one message per five seconds for the ring, and high-rate
motion and respiration telemetry at ten messages per second for the
clothing, reflecting the general pattern that inertial sensing operates at
rates well above episodic wellness metrics
\cite{seneviratne2017wearables}. The precise schemas and rates are normative
interfaces of the artefact and are specified in
Chapter~\ref{ch:architecture}.

\subsection{Why Synthetic Workloads}
\label{sec:bg-wearables-synthetic}

\noindent The evaluation drives the gateway with \emph{synthetic} telemetry
produced by a deterministic, seedable simulator rather than with live
devices, for four reasons. First, privacy and ethics: real wearable
telemetry is personal health data, and collecting it from human subjects
would impose consent and data-protection obligations disproportionate to
research questions that concern the platform, not the people. Second,
access: as noted above, commercial wearables typically expose their data
through vendor applications and clouds rather than through local interfaces
a household gateway could subscribe to \cite{seneviratne2017wearables}, so a
faithful live pipeline would measure a vendor integration more than the
platform. Third, reproducibility: a seeded generator makes the workload a
versioned artefact --- the same seed reproduces the same per-device payload
sequence bit for bit --- which is the precondition for the repetition and
comparison discipline that benchmarking methodology demands
\cite{varghese2021survey}. Fourth, controllability: load sweeps, deliberately
invalid payloads and device-silence windows can be injected on schedule,
which no live deployment provides on demand.

The cost of this choice is value realism: synthetic measurements are drawn
from plausible ranges, not from physiological models, and no claim in this
work depends on the clinical meaning of the values. The platform costs under
study --- validation, deduplication, twin updates, resource consumption ---
are driven by message rate, size and schema complexity rather than by the
sampled values, which is why rate fidelity was prioritised over value
fidelity. This limitation and its implications are revisited with the
threats to validity in Section~\ref{sec:eval-validity}.

\section{Embedded Linux Build Systems for Edge Gateways}
\label{sec:bg-yocto}

\noindent A gateway that claims to be trustworthy infrastructure should be
able to answer a simple question: \emph{exactly what software is running on
it, and could an independent party rebuild it?} How its operating system is
produced determines whether that question is answerable. Three families of
approaches exist for putting Linux on an edge device.

The first is a \emph{generic binary distribution} (Debian and derivatives
being typical), installed from vendor images and provisioned by hand or by
scripts. This is the fastest route to a working system, but the result is
imperative rather than declarative: the installed system is the accumulation
of package operations and manual configuration, its exact contents are not
derivable from a versioned description, and two nominally identical
installations diverge over time. Unused components enlarge both footprint
and attack surface.

The second family is exemplified by \emph{Buildroot}, which its manual
describes as ``a simple, efficient and easy-to-use tool to generate embedded
Linux systems through cross-compilation'' \cite{buildroot2026manual}. From a
single configuration, Buildroot cross-compiles a toolchain, kernel and root
filesystem, yielding small images with modest learning effort. Its
configuration is, however, monolithic: there is no first-class layering
mechanism through which board support, a middleware stack and
project-specific additions can be maintained and versioned as independent,
reusable units.

The third family is the \emph{Yocto Project}, an open-source build framework
in which a target image is described by declarative recipes organised into
layers, evaluated by the BitBake engine \cite{yocto2024scarthgap}. Three
properties of this model carry the weight of the choice made in this work.
First, \emph{composability}: hardware support, upstream package sets and
project-specific customisation live in separate layers, so the gateway image
is expressed as a small custom layer (\texttt{meta-egw}) on top of unmodified
upstream layers. Second, \emph{reproducibility}: every input layer can be
pinned to an exact revision --- in this project through a \texttt{kas}
manifest --- so that a documented checkout rebuilds the same system, which
converts the platform itself into a verifiable artefact rather than an
environmental assumption. Third, \emph{longevity}: the project maintains
designated long-term-support release lines; this work uses the Scarthgap
(5.0) line, released in April 2024 and supported until April 2028
\cite{yocto2024scarthgap}, so the platform's base remains maintained beyond
the dissertation's horizon. A further, operational consequence separates the
image-built families from binary distributions: an image-built system is
deployed and updated as a whole artefact, so the state of a device is always
one of a small set of known, buildable images, whereas package-managed
systems evolve in place, one package transaction at a time, into states that
no one ever built or tested as a unit. For a gateway whose evaluation
depends on knowing exactly what was running when a measurement was taken,
the image-based model is the one that keeps the evidence chain intact. The cost is non-trivial build complexity and
build time, accepted here because platform reproducibility is one of the
claims under evaluation (RQ1, Section~\ref{sec:intro-rqs}), not an incidental
property. It should be noted that much of the directly comparative
Yocto-versus-Buildroot material available is vendor-authored; under the
review protocol it is treated as grey literature and engineering context
only, and the comparison above is therefore argued from the projects' own
primary documentation \cite{yocto2024scarthgap,buildroot2026manual} rather
than from third-party benchmarks.

\section{Containers on Constrained ARM Hardware}
\label{sec:bg-containers}

\noindent The service layer of the gateway is packaged as \ac{OCI}
containers: services are packaged with their dependencies into
architecture-specific images that can be pinned by digest, which extends the
reproducibility argument from the operating system to the deployed stack. Two
properties of the \ac{OCI} distribution model do the work here. An image
digest is a content hash, so a digest-pinned reference denotes immutable
bytes --- unlike a tag, which can be re-pointed silently --- and a published
image can carry per-architecture variants under one name, which makes
``native \texttt{linux/arm64}'' a verifiable property of each pinned image
rather than an assumption. In the artefact, every service image is pinned by
digest in a versioned lock file, so the deployed stack, like the operating
system beneath it, is described entirely by content under version control
(Chapter~\ref{ch:architecture}).
The viability of this choice on small ARM devices is an empirical question
with an established literature. Felter et al.\ compare virtual machines and
Linux containers on server-class hardware and establish the baseline
expectation that container overhead is modest relative to full
virtualisation \cite{felter2015updated}. Morabito evaluates container
technologies specifically on \ac{IoT}-class edge devices, including ARM
single-board computers, and concludes that containerisation is viable on
constrained ARM hardware \cite{morabito2017virtualization}. More recently,
Gamess and Parajuli benchmark Docker across several Raspberry Pi
generations, reporting that even low-end models such as the Raspberry Pi~3
family can run a Docker engine acceptably, with limited \ac{RAM} capacity
--- rather than processing overhead --- the most frequently encountered
constraint \cite{gamess2023docker}.

Two implications follow for this work. First, the memory finding is
directly actionable: the twin stack includes a \ac{JSON} document database
and a JVM-based twin platform, so per-container \ac{RAM} profiling under
load is a first-class metric of the evaluation (RQ3), not an afterthought.
Second, the published evidence concerns container runtimes in general, not
this stack: the resource measurements of Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}
therefore characterise the cost of the specific composition used here ---
broker, controller, twin services and database --- rather than re-deriving
the general overhead results. Orchestration platforms beyond a single-node
container composition (such as lightweight Kubernetes distributions) are
out of scope: the artefact is a single-gateway deployment, and no research
question involves multi-node scheduling.

\section{Interoperability: MQTT and the Web of Things}
\label{sec:bg-mqtt}

\subsection{MQTT and Telemetry Ingestion}
\label{sec:bg-mqtt-protocol}

\noindent \ac{MQTT} is a lightweight publish/subscribe protocol
standardised by OASIS; version 5.0 is the current OASIS Standard
\cite{oasis2019mqtt}. Its design properties --- a compact wire format,
hierarchical topics, decoupling of producers from consumers through a
broker, and graded delivery guarantees --- have made it the de facto
ingestion protocol for device telemetry. The delivery semantics matter
architecturally: \ac{QoS}~1 (at-least-once) matches the reliability need of
telemetry ingestion, but explicitly permits duplicate delivery, so a
correct receiver must be idempotent. This single protocol property
propagates through the entire artefact: it is why every event carries a
deterministic message identifier and sequence number, and why the
controller enforces run-scoped deduplication before touching any twin
(Chapter~\ref{ch:architecture}).

The choice of \ac{QoS}~1 among the standard's three delivery levels
\cite{oasis2019mqtt} is itself an engineering trade-off. \ac{QoS}~0 (at most
once) silently drops messages under disturbance, which would make delivery
rate --- a primary metric of this evaluation --- unobservable at the
protocol level. \ac{QoS}~2 (exactly once) removes duplicates at the
transport, but at the cost of an additional acknowledgement exchange per
message, and its guarantee ends at the \ac{MQTT} client boundary: a
crash-and-redeliver cycle between the subscribing client and the twin
platform could still apply the same event twice. Since the receiving side
must therefore implement application-level idempotency in any case, paying
\ac{QoS}~2's per-message overhead buys little, and \ac{QoS}~1 plus
deterministic deduplication is the economical design --- an argument that
recurs in the controller design of Chapter~\ref{ch:architecture}.

The broker is a deployment choice with measurable consequences: Koziolek et
al.\ compare \ac{MQTT} brokers for distributed edge deployments and find
substantial differences in throughput and loss behaviour between
implementations \cite{koziolek2020mqtt}. Mosquitto, used in this work, is
attractive for a single-node edge deployment because of its small footprint
and operational simplicity; broker selection is not itself under evaluation,
and the experimental protocol treats the broker as a fixed component of the
composition.

\subsection{The Web of Things}
\label{sec:bg-wot}

\noindent Interoperability above the transport requires agreement on what a
device's data \emph{means}. The W3C \ac{WoT} \ac{TD}~1.1 Recommendation
defines a standard, machine-readable format for describing a thing's
capabilities --- its properties, actions and events, together with the
protocol bindings through which they are reachable \cite{w3c2023wottd}. In
this work each wearable type has a \ac{TD} aligned with its \ac{JSON}
Schema, so the twin's data model is documented in a standard form rather
than only in project-specific schemas; the two artefacts are kept
consistent by construction
(Chapter~\ref{ch:architecture}). The choice is reinforced by the platform:
Eclipse Ditto documents native \ac{WoT} integration, stable and active by
default since version 3.0.0 \cite{eclipse2026ditto}, making the \ac{TD} the
natural convergence point between the wearable schemas and the twin
platform's own description of things.

\section{Self-Sovereign Identity as Context}
\label{sec:bg-ssi}

\noindent The broader C2DTA vision includes decentralised identity and
verifiable interactions between consumer agents. In this dissertation,
\ac{SSI} appears exclusively as architectural context: the evaluated
platform neither implements nor depends on identity agents, ledgers or
credentials, and no statement in this document should be read as an
\ac{SSI} or blockchain result. The delimitation is deliberate: the research
questions (Section~\ref{sec:intro-rqs}) are answerable without identity
components, and the project plan classifies such components as strictly
conditional, non-essential scope.

The comparison axes of Table~\ref{tab:related-work} are not arbitrary: each
operationalises one link of the evidence chain this work aims to close. A
reproducible operating-system build fixes the platform's provenance; a
native ARM64 target keeps the deployment representative of edge-class
hardware rather than of development machines; an off-the-shelf twin platform
distinguishes reusable results from bespoke-engine results; multi-wearable
ingestion makes the workload representative of a household rather than of a
single-device demonstration; and a pre-specified protocol, frozen before
data collection and published with its artefacts, is what allows any of the
resulting numbers to be checked. The
pattern across the rows is consistent: each related system contributes one
or two of the defining properties, and none of the examined works combines a
reproducibly built operating platform, a native ARM64 target, an
off-the-shelf twin platform and concurrent multi-wearable ingestion under a
pre-specified evaluation protocol. In summary: component-level
evidence exists for edge digital twins \cite{picone2023flexible}, broker
behaviour \cite{koziolek2020mqtt} and container overhead on constrained
devices \cite{morabito2017virtualization,felter2015updated,gamess2023docker},
and the C2DTA line provides the architectural vision \cite{pinto2026c2dta};
but, among the sources examined in this preliminary review --- assembled from
seed references and snowballing, prior to execution of the registered search
protocol of Chapter~3 --- no published study was identified that composes
these elements into a reproducible, Yocto-built ARM64 gateway with a
pre-specified multi-scenario evaluation of wearable digital-twin ingestion.
That composition is the subject of the remaining chapters, and the claim
remains deliberately bounded: it is relative to the sources examined so far,
and will be re-stated against the full protocol once the institutional
database searches have been executed.
